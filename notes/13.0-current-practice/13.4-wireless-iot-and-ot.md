# 13.4 Wireless Details, IoT Protocols, and the Purdue Model

Section **13.0 Current practice**. WPA3 and the 802.11 generations are in 4.4 and 10.4. Cellular as a WAN backup is in 8.2. IoT isolation and SCADA are in 11.2 and 4.5. This note is the open-Wi-Fi encryption, the sleep schedule, 5G slices, the two sensor protocols, and the industrial level model.

## Opportunistic Wireless Encryption

**OWE** (RFC 8110) is what the Wi-Fi Alliance calls **Enhanced Open**. The network has no passphrase. Each client still does a Diffie-Hellman exchange with the AP and then encrypts its unicast frames.

People on the same SSID cannot casually read each other's traffic the way they can on a classic open network. That is the gain.

OWE does not authenticate the AP and does not authenticate the client. An evil twin can offer the same open name and complete its own exchange. A captive portal still does not prove identity (12.5). Use OWE for a guest or a café network where a shared passphrase would be printed on the wall anyway. Use WPA3-Enterprise where you need to know who joined.

## Target Wake Time

**Target Wake Time** (TWT) is part of **802.11ax** (Wi-Fi 6). The AP and the client agree on a schedule. The client wakes to send or receive, then sleeps. The radio is the expensive part of a battery, so this matters for phones and sensors.

Two forms exist. An **individual** agreement is one client and the AP. A **broadcast** agreement is a group of clients sharing a wake window. Either way, a client that misses the agreement and sleeps through its window looks "associated" and does not answer. When a Wi-Fi 6 sensor stops checking in, look at the power-save state before you replace the sensor.

TWT also reduces contention: fewer stations are awake to talk over each other. It does not add throughput for a laptop that is already awake and transferring a file.

## 5G network slicing

A **slice** is a logical network on top of one physical 5G network, from the radio through the core. Each slice can have its own capacity, latency target, and isolation. A phone or a sensor is placed on a slice by policy.

The three service types the standards name:

| Slice type | Built for |
|---|---|
| eMBB | Capacity. Video, broadband, the everyday phone |
| URLLC | Very low latency and high reliability. Control loops, some industrial and vehicle uses |
| mMTC | A huge number of small, infrequent devices. Meters and sensors |

An enterprise slice, or a **private 5G** network on the site, is how a plant gets cellular coverage that is not the public Internet with a different logo. The traffic can stay on the site and land in a specific VLAN or VPN.

Slicing is not a VLAN ID on the SIM and nothing more. The isolation has to hold on the radio scheduler, the transport, and the core. A slice that shares a congested backhaul with the public users will miss a latency promise that exists only on a diagram. LTE and 5G as a failover circuit for a branch (8.2) usually are not a private slice. They are a modem on the public network.

## MQTT

**MQTT** is a publish/subscribe bus for small messages. Devices do not call each other. They talk to a **broker**.

A sensor publishes `plant/line2/temp` with a payload. Anything that has subscribed to that topic receives it. The broker is the availability problem: two sensors cannot talk if the broker is down, so the broker wants the same redundancy as any other service.

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Port | TCP **1883**. MQTT over TLS is **8883** |
| QoS 0 | At most once. The broker does not ack |
| QoS 1 | At least once. Duplicates are possible |
| QoS 2 | Exactly once. More round trips |
| Last will | A message the broker publishes for the client if the client disappears uncleanly |
| Retained message | The broker keeps the latest payload and gives it to a new subscriber immediately |

Use TLS and an account on the broker. An open MQTT broker on the Internet is a public pastebin of your sensors. Keep the broker on the IoT VLAN (11.2), and let only the broker reach the systems that need the data.

## CoAP

**CoAP** (Constrained Application Protocol) is a small request/response protocol for devices that cannot carry HTTP. It looks like a tiny HTTP: GET, PUT, POST, DELETE, and response codes, in a compact binary header.

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Transport | UDP **5683**. **CoAPS** uses DTLS on **5684** |
| Observe | A client asks to be told when a resource changes, which is the subscription-shaped feature |
| Where it runs | Often over 6LoWPAN on low-power radios, then a gateway translates to the LAN |

MQTT is a broker and topics over TCP. CoAP is a client talking to a resource over UDP. A battery sensor that cannot keep a TCP session up is a CoAP candidate. A site that wants many consumers of one reading, and a broker in the middle, is an MQTT candidate. Gateways that speak both are normal. The security question is the same: DTLS or TLS, and the gateway is the boundary, because the sensor itself will be running old firmware forever (11.2).

## The Purdue model

The **Purdue model** (used with ISA-95 and IEC 62443) numbers an industrial network so office traffic has a long path to a controller. SCADA and ICS vocabulary is in 4.5. The levels are the map.

| Level | What lives there |
|---|---|
| 0 | The process. Sensors and actuators on the equipment |
| 1 | Basic control. PLCs and safety controllers. Tight timing |
| 2 | Supervision. HMIs, the local SCADA view operators stand in front of |
| 3 | Site operations. Historians, engineering workstations, the plant schedulers |
| 3.5 | The industrial DMZ. The only planned crossing between the plant and the business |
| 4 | Business IT. Email, file servers, the ERP |
| 5 | The enterprise WAN and, in some drawings, the cloud |

A workstation at level 4 does not open a session straight to a PLC at level 1. Data that the business needs, such as production counts, is pushed to a historian or a broker in the **level 3.5 DMZ**, and the business reads it there. Remote vendors land on a jump host in that DMZ, not on the control network (12.4).

Flat is the failure mode. One VLAN from the front office to the line, because a contractor needed to upload a program, removes every level at once. Put the vendor path in the DMZ, time-limit the account, and log it. Availability outranks convenience here: a firewall rule that blocks a safety controller's legitimate traffic is its own incident, so the allow list has to be built with the people who run the process.
