# 1.7 Unbounded Media

Section **1.0 Networking Fundamentals**.

**Unbounded media** has no copper or glass conductor. The signal is an electromagnetic wave. You gain mobility and you lose a private, predictable cable. Interference, shared spectrum, and range become the design problems.

## Radio

Data is modulated onto a radio frequency. Depending on the band and the country, radio networking sits somewhere from tens of kHz into the hundreds of GHz. Wi-Fi uses the unlicensed **2.4 GHz**, **5 GHz**, and (Wi-Fi 6E / 7) **6 GHz** bands. The same bands are used by microwave ovens, Bluetooth, baby monitors, and neighbors, so radio is inherently exposed to EMI and to other transmitters.

**Spread spectrum** was built so a signal could hide and survive interference by occupying more bandwidth than the raw data needs.

| Method | What it does | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|
| DSSS — direct sequence | Multiplies the data by a faster chipping code so the signal spreads out. Resistant to narrowband noise | 802.11 and 802.11b |
| FHSS — frequency hopping | The carrier jumps among frequencies on a schedule. A jammer has to follow the hops | Bluetooth, older 802.11 |
| OFDM — orthogonal frequency division | Splits the channel into many small subcarriers sent at once | 802.11a/g/n/ac/ax. This is modern Wi-Fi, not a 1990s spread-spectrum trick |

## Infrared

Infrared is light just below visible red, roughly 300 GHz to 400 THz. Serial infrared (SIR), fast infrared (FIR), and very fast infrared (VFIR) are speed grades of the old IrDA link.

- Needs **line of sight**.
- Short range.
- Does not go through walls, which is also its security property: the neighbor cannot hear it.
- Sunlight, smoke, and dust interrupt it.

TV remotes are the everyday example. It is not a building LAN technology.

## Bluetooth

Short-range radio in the **2.4 GHz** band, using frequency hopping. Devices **pair** (authenticate and remember each other) before they exchange useful data.

Range depends on radio class, not on a single "30 feet" rule:

| Class | Power | Rough range |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 mW | Up to about 100 m |
| 2 | 2.5 mW | About 10 m. Most phones and headsets |
| 3 | 1 mW | About 1 m |

Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) is the low-power variant used by sensors and beacons. It is not the same radio schedule as classic Bluetooth, but it shares the 2.4 GHz band.

## Microwave

Microwave links use roughly 300 MHz to 300 GHz (wavelengths from about 1 m down to 1 mm). In networking the usual meaning is a **point-to-point, line-of-sight** shot between two dish antennas, over kilometers. Towers, weather, and Fresnel-zone clearance matter. This is a WAN tool, not a desk tool.

## Wireless access point

An access point (AP, or WAP) lets wireless stations join, and it bridges them onto the wired network. A home "wireless router" is an AP plus a router plus a switch in one box. Those are three jobs. In a business network they are often separate: the AP only bridges, and a wireless LAN controller or the router does the rest.

## SSID

The **service set identifier** is the name of the wireless network. It is a string of up to **32 octets** (usually 32 characters), not a 32-bit number.

The AP advertises the SSID in beacon frames. Hiding the SSID (disabling the broadcast) is not access control. The name is still in probe responses and in client traffic. Change the default name, but do the real protection with WPA2 or WPA3.

## Bringing an AP up

1. Pick hardware that matches the client count, the bands, and the security you need.
2. Place it. High, central, away from metal and microwave ovens. One AP per closet is not a design.
3. Change the administrator password before it touches production.
4. Set SSID, encryption (WPA2-Personal or WPA3, never WEP or open unless you mean it), channel, and power.
5. Prefer not to rely on a hidden SSID.
6. Connect a client and confirm it gets an address and passes traffic.

Channel choice and the 802.11 standards are in 4.4.
