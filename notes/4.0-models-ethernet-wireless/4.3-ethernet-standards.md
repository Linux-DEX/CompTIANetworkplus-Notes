# 4.3 Ethernet Standards

Section **4.0 Models, Ethernet, and Wireless**.

## What Ethernet is

Ethernet is the family of LAN standards that defines the frame, the addresses, and the physical media. Bob Metcalfe built the first version at Xerox PARC in 1973, first called the Alto Aloha Network, then Ethernet, after the old idea of a medium ("luminiferous aether") that carries waves to everyone on it. The shared cable really did deliver every bit to every station.

DIX (Digital, Intel, Xerox) published the first spec. **IEEE 802.3** is the international standard. People say "Ethernet" for both. 802.3 added the length field interpretation and a few frame details; on the wire, a modern frame is the DIX shape with an EtherType.

IEEE is the standards body (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers). The 802 family covers LAN and MAN technologies: 802.3 is Ethernet, 802.11 is Wi-Fi, 802.1Q is VLAN tagging.

Early Ethernet used CSMA/CD on coax (2.2). Switched full-duplex Ethernet keeps the frame format and drops the shared-medium rules.

## How the names work

```text
[speed] BASE [medium]
```

| Piece | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | 10, 100, 1000, 10G | Megabits per second, unless it already says G |
| BASE | BASE | Baseband: one signal uses the whole medium (2.1) |
| Medium | 5, 2, T, TX, SX, LX | The cable, and sometimes the wavelength |

BROAD, when you see it in old names, means broadband. You will not meet it in current Ethernet.

| Name | Read it as |
|---|---|
| 10BASE5 | 10 Mbps, baseband, 500 m coax segment |
| 10BASE2 | 10 Mbps, baseband, about 185 m thin coax. The "2" is "about 200 m" |
| 10BASE-T | 10 Mbps, baseband, twisted pair |
| 100BASE-TX | 100 Mbps, baseband, two pairs of Cat 5. The X marks the line code (4B/5B), not "full duplex" |
| 1000BASE-T | 1 Gbps, all four pairs |
| 1000BASE-SX | 1 Gbps, short-wavelength fiber (850 nm), multimode |
| 10GBASE-SR | 10 Gbps, short reach, multimode |
| 10GBASE-LR | 10 Gbps, long reach, single-mode (1310 nm) |
| 10GBASE-ER | 10 Gbps, extended reach, single-mode (1550 nm) |

The trailing letter is a wavelength or a cable family. It is not a duplex flag. Duplex is a separate setting.

## The generations

### Thicknet — 10BASE5

The original. Thick coax, RG-8, **50 ohm**, 10 Mbps, **500 m** per segment. Stations attached with a vampire tap and an AUI cable to an external transceiver. Half duplex, CSMA/CD, terminator at each end. Historical. Know the distance and the cable, not how to install it.

### Thinnet — 10BASE2

RG-58, 50 ohm, 10 Mbps, **185 m**, BNC T-connectors, terminator on the two ends. Cheaper than thicknet, so it was called cheapernet. Still a single shared bus: one bad T-connector took down the segment.

### 10BASE-T

1990. 10 Mbps on two pairs of Category 3 or better, **100 m**. The star replaces the bus. A hub keeps the logical bus (one collision domain). A switch gives each port its own collision domain. This is the oldest Ethernet you might still see, and only on neglected gear.

### Fast Ethernet — 100BASE-TX and 100BASE-FX

**100BASE-TX.** IEEE 802.3u. 100 Mbps, two pairs, Cat 5, 100 m. It replaced 10BASE-T without repulling the Cat 5 that buildings already had. Half or full duplex. Full duplex needs a switch, not a hub.

**100BASE-FX.** 100 Mbps on two strands of multimode fiber, one each direction. About **400 m** half duplex (collision detection still has to cross the link) and **2 km** full duplex. The duplex gap is the exam point: the distance limit is not one number.

### Gigabit — 1000BASE-T and the fiber variants

**1000BASE-T.** IEEE 802.3ab. 1 Gbps, **all four pairs**, Cat 5e or better, 100 m. Each pair is used in both directions at once. Autonegotiation is mandatory. A cable with one broken pair can still link at 100 Mbps and then confuse you, because 100BASE-TX only needs two pairs.

| Name | Medium | Reach (typical) |
|---|---|---|
| 1000BASE-T | Cat 5e/6, four pairs | 100 m |
| 1000BASE-TX | Cat 6, two pairs only | 100 m. A commercial failure. Do not confuse it with 1000BASE-T |
| 1000BASE-CX | Shielded copper | 25 m. In-rack, obsolete |
| 1000BASE-SX | Multimode, 850 nm | 220–550 m depending on the fiber grade |
| 1000BASE-LX | Single-mode or multimode, 1310 nm | 5 km single-mode, about 550 m multimode |

### 10 Gigabit

| Name | Medium | Reach |
|---|---|---|
| 10GBASE-T | Cat 6a (100 m) or Cat 6 (about 55 m). IEEE 802.3an. Four pairs | 100 m on 6a |
| 10GBASE-SR | Multimode, short reach, 850 nm | About 300 m on OM3, longer on OM4 |
| 10GBASE-LR | Single-mode, 1310 nm | 10 km |
| 10GBASE-ER | Single-mode, 1550 nm | 40 km |
| 10GBASE-SW | Multimode, framed to ride a SONET/SDH WAN | WAN PHY. The "W" is the WAN encoding, not a distance |

SR / LR / ER are LAN PHYs. SW / LW / EW are the same optics with a WAN framing. If the question says SONET, it wants the W.

### Faster, so the names are familiar

40GBASE and 100GBASE use multiple lanes (four fibers, or four wavelengths) and QSFP modules (1.6). The naming rule does not change: speed, BASE, reach letter.

## Rules that apply to all of them

**The channel is 100 m on copper**, not because the electrons get tired, but because attenuation, crosstalk, and (on half duplex) the slot time were specified for that length. Patch cords count.

**Autonegotiation** picks speed and duplex from what both ends advertise. If you hard-set one end to 100/full and leave the other on auto, the auto side typically falls back to **half** duplex. The result is late collisions and awful throughput on a link that still "works". Set both ends the same way, or leave both on auto.

**A frame** is between 64 and 1518 bytes on the wire (destination MAC through FCS), which is a 1500-byte payload. Smaller frames are dropped as runts. A VLAN tag adds 4 bytes (802.1Q), so the baby jumbo of 1522 is normal on a trunk. Details of the header fields are in 4.2.
