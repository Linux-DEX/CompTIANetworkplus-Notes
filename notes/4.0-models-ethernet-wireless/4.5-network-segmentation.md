# 4.5 Network Segmentation

Section **4.0 Models, Ethernet, and Wireless**.

**Segmentation** divides one network into smaller networks. Each piece is a **segment**, with a boundary you can point at. The segments can still communicate, but only through a device you control (a router, a firewall, a layer-3 switch), so "can communicate" is a decision instead of an accident.

## Physical and logical

**Physical segmentation** uses separate gear: different switches, different routers, different cables. The boundary is a box. A group of machines that share one switch and have no uplink to anything else are physically segmented, whether or not you meant them to be.

**Logical segmentation** carves segments out of shared gear. The usual tool is a **VLAN**: one physical switch behaves as several independent switches. Ports in VLAN 10 cannot send a frame to ports in VLAN 20 unless a router routes between them. The cables do not move. The configuration does. Trunks (802.1Q) carry several VLANs on one uplink by tagging each frame.

Physical and logical solve different problems. Two VLANs on one switch share that switch's fate if the switch dies or is compromised at the management plane. Two switches do not.

## Why segment

**Security.** A user VLAN that can ARP every server is one stolen laptop away from the database. Put the servers in their own segment and allow only the ports the application needs.

**Containment of something you cannot patch.** A **legacy** host that still runs a critical application, and that no longer receives security fixes, does not belong on the same segment as the mail server. Isolation is the compensation for the missing patches.

**Broadcast and congestion control.** Broadcasts stop at a router. A segment per department keeps one team's broadcast chatter, and one team's bulk transfer, off everyone else's links. Smaller broadcast domains are also smaller failure domains when something starts flooding.

**Load distribution.** Two servers in two segments, with a load balancer in front (1.9), or simply two groups of users on two uplinks, so one link is not the whole company.

**Compliance scope.** The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) applies to any system that stores, processes, or transmits cardholder data. Segmentation is **not** itself a PCI requirement. It shrinks the set of systems the assessor has to review, because a properly isolated cardholder segment is the only place that data can reach. The boundary to the rest of the network, and to the Internet, gets the firewall and the IDS. A flat network makes every PC in scope. Assessors, and the bill, follow the scope.

**A place for public servers.** Staff systems stay on an internal segment. Servers that the Internet must reach (web, mail) go on a separate segment, traditionally a **DMZ** (screened subnet), between two firewall policies: the Internet can reach the DMZ on specific ports, and the DMZ cannot initiate connections into the internal network. A compromised web server then has nowhere useful to go.

**A place to break things.** Patch tests and new builds belong on a lab segment. An outage there costs a rebuild. The same outage on the production segment costs the business.

## Industrial networks

**ICS** (industrial control systems) is the family name for the equipment that runs plants, pipelines, water systems, and power. **SCADA** (supervisory control and data acquisition) is the ICS style used across a wide area: sensors turn a physical measurement into a signal, a controller (PLC or RTU) acts on it, and a SCADA host shows the operators the picture and sends commands back.

That network is not a place for a laptop that also reads email. Segment it from the business LAN, allow only the specific historian or jump host that operators need, and put a firewall on the boundary. The stakes are physical, not just data loss. The level numbers for that boundary are the Purdue model in 13.4.

## Honeypots

A **honeypot** is a decoy built to be attacked. It offers nothing the business needs, it logs everything, and it is easier to break into than the real server so an attacker spends time there instead of on production. The logs are for understanding the technique and, sometimes, for an investigation.

A **honeynet** is a segment of more than one honeypot, wired so it looks like a normal small network. It has to be segmented on purpose: a decoy that can route into the real LAN is not a decoy, it is a door.

Honeypots are a detection and research tool. They are not a substitute for patching the real servers.

## What a segment is not

A new subnet on the same VLAN, with no filter between them, is just a larger flat network plus an extra router. Segmentation counts when a policy device can refuse traffic. VLANs without an ACL between them only stop broadcasts. They do not stop a host that is willing to send a packet to the router.
