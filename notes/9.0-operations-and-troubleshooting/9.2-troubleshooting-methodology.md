# 9.2 Troubleshooting Methodology

Section **9.0 Operations and Troubleshooting**.

CompTIA's order is the order. Skipping to "I will just change the mask and see" creates a second fault while the first one is still there.

1. **Identify the problem.** What works, what does not, and since when. One user or every user. One application or every application. Ask what changed. Reproduce it if you can do that without making it worse. Write down the symptom in the user's words and then in yours.
2. **Establish a theory.** Pick the most likely cause that fits the scope. A single desk is a cable, a port, or that PC. A floor is a switch or a VLAN. A site is the WAN or the firewall. Do not start with the WAN when one person cannot print.
3. **Test the theory.** The smallest test that would have to fail if you are right. Ping the gateway from that PC. Check the port counter. Move the PC to a known-good jack. If the theory is wrong, say so and form the next one. Do not stack changes.
4. **Plan the fix.** What you will change, what it might break, and how you will undo it. A fix that needs a change window is still a fix. Do not smuggle it in as a test.
5. **Implement**, one change at a time, or you will not know which change mattered.
6. **Verify** the user's actual task, not only your ping. Then check that you did not break the neighbor.
7. **Document** the symptom, the cause, and the fix. The next person, who may be you in six months, will search for it.

## Scope narrows the layer

| Scope | Look first |
|---|---|
| One application, one host | The application, DNS for that name, the firewall rule for that port |
| One host, every application | Address, mask, gateway, VLAN, cable, NIC, duplex |
| Every host on one switch | Uplink, VLAN allowed on the trunk, the switch itself, DHCP for that VLAN |
| Every host at one site | WAN, default route, the site firewall, the DHCP or DNS server if the failure is names or new leases |
| Every site | The shared thing: the data center, the identity provider, the public DNS, a bad change you just pushed everywhere |

Bottom-up (cable, then VLAN, then IP, then name, then application) is the right direction when the symptom is "no connection". Top-down is reasonable when the user can browse and cannot reach one internal app. Either way you are testing a theory, not reciting the OSI model at the user.

## While you are in there

- Note the time. Logs are useless if you do not know when it started.
- Do not reboot as the first step on a shared device. You destroy the state you needed, and you drop everyone else.
- A workaround (a static route, a disabled NIC, a hosts-file entry) is acceptable to restore service. It is not the resolution until you have removed it or documented why it stays.
- If you cannot reproduce it, say that. An intermittent you cannot see is a monitoring problem (9.1), not a reason to replace random hardware.
