# 9.5 Change Management

Section **9.0 Operations and Troubleshooting**.

A change is anything you do on purpose that can affect service: a VLAN, a route, a firewall rule, a firmware upgrade, a new AP, a cable move. Rebooting a core switch because you felt like it is a change. It is not a troubleshooting step.

## The pieces

| Piece | What it is for |
|---|---|
| Request | What will change, why, and which systems it touches |
| Approval | Someone who is allowed to accept the risk says yes. On a small network that may be one person. It is still a decision, not a surprise |
| Maintenance window | When it is allowed to hurt. Not during payroll, not during the broadcast, not "whenever I am online" |
| Plan | The steps, in order, including how you will confirm it worked |
| Backout | How you return to the last known-good state, and how long that takes. If you cannot describe the backout, you are not ready |
| Test | What "working" means, checked after the change, by someone other than the step that made it if you can |
| Documentation | The diagram, the IP plan, and the ticket updated the same day |

**Emergency changes** skip the wait, not the record. You write it down while you do it or immediately after, and someone reviews it. An emergency that happens every week is a process failure.

## What usually goes wrong

- The change worked on the switch and broke the thing the requester did not mention. Scope the request in the user's language: "which application, from where".
- Two changes in one window. When it breaks, you do not know which one. One change, or a written dependency between them.
- No backup of the config before the upgrade. The backout is now "rebuild from memory".
- The window ends and you are mid-change. The backout time is part of the window. Start early enough to undo.
- Nobody told the help desk. The first hour of the incident is users reporting the change you are still making.

A **change freeze** (no non-emergency changes) around a known critical period is a normal control. It is not bureaucracy. It is how you avoid debugging a firewall and a WAN cutover at the same time.

Configuration management is the technical half. Store the configs, diff them, and be able to say what changed since Tuesday. A device whose only copy of the truth is its running memory will lie to you after the next power cut, by forgetting.
