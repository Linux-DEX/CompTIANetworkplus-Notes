# 8.3 Remote Access and VPNs

Section **8.0 WAN and Remote Access**.

Remote access means a person or a site that is not on your LAN can use something on it, without you pretending the Internet is a private wire.

## Methods, from weakest to the one you deploy

| Method | What crosses the network | Use it? |
|---|---|---|
| Telnet, FTP, HTTP admin | Passwords and session contents in the clear | No. Replace with SSH, SFTP, HTTPS |
| SSH, RDP, HTTPS to a single host | One encrypted application | Fine for administrators. RDP belongs behind a VPN or an RD Gateway, not open on 3389 to the world |
| Remote-access VPN | The user's traffic, encrypted to a concentrator, then onto the LAN | The normal way a laptop joins the office |
| Site-to-site VPN | A standing tunnel between two routers | The normal way two offices share a network over the Internet |
| Dial-up, ISDN | A phone call to a modem bank | Historical, or a last-ditch out-of-band path |
| Out-of-band | A separate modem, cellular, or console server that works when the data network does not | How you fix a router you can no longer reach |

**Split tunnel** means only traffic for the office goes into the VPN. The user's YouTube goes out their home ISP. It saves the office's bandwidth and it means the office firewall does not see the user's Internet traffic. **Full tunnel** sends everything to the office first. Use full tunnel when policy says all traffic must be inspected. Use split tunnel when you have decided that trade on purpose, not because it was the wizard's default.

## What a VPN provides

| Property | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Confidentiality | Encryption. Someone on the path sees ciphertext |
| Integrity | A change to the packet is detected |
| Authentication | You know which device or which user opened the tunnel. A shared password is weaker than a certificate or a per-user login |
| Anti-replay | A captured packet cannot be sent again later and accepted |

A VPN does not make the far computer trustworthy. A laptop on the VPN is a machine on your LAN. Posture checks (is the disk encrypted, is it patched) exist because of that.

## Protocols

| Protocol | What it is |
|---|---|
| IPSec | The standard for site-to-site and for many remote-access clients. Details in 10.2. AH or ESP, IKE on UDP 500, NAT-T on UDP 4500 |
| SSL/TLS VPN | The tunnel rides TLS, often on TCP 443, sometimes UDP. Works from networks that only allow web traffic. Can be a full tunnel or a portal that publishes a few web apps |
| GRE | A generic tunnel. It carries multicast and routing protocols. It does **not** encrypt. Pair it with IPSec if the path is untrusted |
| L2TP | A tunneling protocol, often combined with IPSec for the encryption (L2TP/IPSec). UDP 1701, plus the IPSec ports |
| PPTP | Old. TCP 1723 and GRE. The authentication has known failures. Do not deploy it |
| WireGuard, OpenVPN | Common modern remote-access implementations. Know that they exist and that each needs its own port allowed. The exam's vocabulary is still IPSec and SSL VPN |
| DMVPN | Multipoint GRE plus a way for spokes to find each other (8.2) |

A **VPN concentrator** is the box (or the firewall function) that terminates many remote-access tunnels. It authenticates the user, assigns an address from a pool that does not overlap the user's home LAN, and applies the ACL for what that user may reach. Overlapping addresses are the usual failure: the user is at home on `192.168.1.0/24` and the office is also `192.168.1.0/24`. The tunnel comes up and then nothing is reachable, because the laptop thinks the office's servers are local. Change one of the two ranges, or the client has to use a pool and a route that cannot collide.

## Building a site-to-site tunnel

1. Interesting traffic: which subnets on each side may enter the tunnel. They must not overlap.
2. Peer addresses: the public addresses of the two gateways. They must be reachable without the tunnel. Do not route the peer address through the tunnel you are trying to build.
3. Matching crypto: same IKE version, same proposals, same pre-shared key or certificates. A mismatch fails at negotiation, before any user packet flows.
4. NAT exemption: traffic that enters the tunnel must not also be PAT'd. Otherwise the far side sees your public address and the proxy identity, not the LAN subnet it has a route for.
5. Routes: each side needs a route for the far LAN, pointing at the tunnel. A default route is not a substitute if the default already points at the Internet.

Out-of-band access is part of this design. When you ship a bad ACL to the concentrator, the VPN is how you were going to fix it. A console server or a cellular modem that does not depend on that ACL is how you actually fix it.

## Where one laptop's packets go

The laptop is at home on `192.168.1.20`. The office file server is `10.0.0.10`. The concentrator gives the tunnel an address `10.8.0.20` and a route to `10.0.0.0/8`.

| Destination | Split tunnel | Full tunnel |
|---|---|---|
| `10.0.0.10` | Into the tunnel, then onto the office LAN | Into the tunnel |
| `1.1.1.1` (a public DNS resolver) | Out the home router, never encrypted by you | Into the tunnel, then out the office firewall |
| `192.168.1.1` (the home router) | Stays on the home LAN. The tunnel does not swallow the local subnet | Same, if the client was configured with a local exemption. If it was not, the user loses the home printer for the duration |

Full tunnel is how you inspect the user's Internet traffic on the office firewall. It also sends their video through your WAN. Split tunnel avoids that, and it means a process on the laptop can talk to the Internet and to the file server at the same time, which is the path a compromised laptop uses to carry data out. Pick it because the policy said so.

**The overlap.** The office also uses `192.168.1.0/24`, and the user tries to reach a printer at `192.168.1.50` that lives at the office. The laptop looks at its own mask, decides `192.168.1.50` is local, and ARPs for it on the home LAN. The packet never enters the tunnel. The tunnel status says connected. Moving the office, or the home, off `192.168.1.0/24` is the fix. A pool of `10.8.0.0/24` for VPN clients does not collide with either LAN, and a route for the office's real subnets points at the tunnel.

**Clientless** is a browser session to a portal (12.4). The user reaches a web app and does not get a route to `10.0.0.0/8`. **Client-to-site** is the laptop above. **Site-to-site** has no user: two routers keep the tunnel up, and the PCs at each site use their ordinary gateway. The PCs do not know a VPN exists. If the tunnel drops, those PCs simply cannot reach the other building, and their Internet may still work.
