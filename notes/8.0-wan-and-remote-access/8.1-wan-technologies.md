# 8.1 WAN Technologies

Section **8.0 WAN and Remote Access**.

A WAN connects sites you do not own the cable between. You buy a service. The demarc is where the carrier's responsibility ends and yours begins. Everything on your side of the demarc, including the CSU/DSU or the modem they may have left there, needs an owner in your documentation.

## How WAN links are shaped

| Service | What you are buying | What to remember |
|---|---|---|
| Leased line | A private circuit, reserved bandwidth, point to point. T1 (1.544 Mbps), E1 (2.048 Mbps), T3, and their optical descendants | You pay for the capacity whether you fill it or not. The CSU/DSU terminates the carrier's signal and hands you a serial or Ethernet interface |
| Circuit switching | A path built per call, then torn down. PSTN, ISDN | ISDN BRI is 2B+D (two 64 kbps bearer channels and a 16 kbps signaling channel). PRI is 23B+D in North America. Dial-on-demand backup, mostly historical |
| Packet switching | A shared carrier network. You send packets or frames. Frame Relay, ATM, and then MPLS | You share the core. Your logical link is a virtual circuit, not a wire of your own |
| Metro Ethernet | The carrier hands you an Ethernet port. The WAN looks like a big switch or a routed link | The common modern handoff. You still do not control the middle |
| Internet VPN | You buy plain Internet at each site and encrypt between them | Cheaper, and the path quality is whatever the Internet is that day. See 8.3 |
| Dark fiber | A fiber with no carrier electronics on it. You light it | You own the failure modes, including the optics |

**CIR** (committed information rate) is the bandwidth the carrier promises on a packet-switched link. Bursts above it can be marked discard-eligible and dropped when the core is busy. The port speed and the CIR are different numbers. A 100 Mbps port with a 20 Mbps CIR will not behave like a 100 Mbps leased line.

## T1, the one leased line the exam still describes

A T1 is 24 channels of 64 kbps plus framing, 1.544 Mbps. It uses two pairs and, traditionally, a CSU/DSU. Encoding and line code (B8ZS, ESF) have to match the carrier. When they do not, the link is up at layer 1 and useless above it, or it flaps. A smartjack at the demarc lets the carrier loop the circuit and test their side without entering your closet.

## WDM

Wavelength-division multiplexing puts several colors of light on one fiber. **CWDM** is the coarse grid, fewer channels, cheaper optics, metro distances. **DWDM** is the dense grid, tens to hundreds of channels, long haul and the core. A mux combines them. An add-drop multiplexer can peel one wavelength off at a site and let the rest continue. This is how a leased "10 Gbps wave" exists on a fiber that is also carrying other customers.

## MPLS

Multiprotocol Label Switching forwards on a short label instead of looking up the IP destination at every hop. The ingress router classifies the packet and pushes a label. Core routers swap labels. The egress pops the label and delivers a normal packet.

What you buy from a carrier as "MPLS" is usually a private IP network: each site gets a port, you advertise your routes (often BGP) into a virtual routing table (**VRF**), and other customers in the same physical core cannot see your table. It is not encryption. It is separation. If the data is sensitive and the threat model includes the carrier, you still encrypt.

**MPLS classes** (traffic engineering, different labels for voice and data) are how the carrier offers QoS. Your marking (DSCP) has to match the contract or the voice packets ride the same class as the backups.

## Administration

- Know the circuit ID, the carrier phone number, and which device is the demarc. A ticket that says "the Internet is down" without a circuit ID waits longer.
- Monitor the handoff, not only the LAN. A WAN can be down while every LAN switch is green.
- One default route per site, toward the WAN, plus a more specific route for the other sites if you are not trusting a full table.
- Decide, in writing, whether a site may fail open onto a cheaper Internet VPN when the MPLS port dies. That is a routing and a security decision, and it should be made before the outage.

## One site, from the desk to the carrier

A branch has a router, a switch, and twenty PCs. The WAN is a carrier Ethernet circuit with a 100 Mbps port and a **20 Mbps CIR**.

```text
PCs — switch — your router — your CSU or NID — demarc — carrier network — headquarters
```

The **demarc** is a jack or a smartjack in the closet. On your side of it, a dead router is your ticket. On the carrier's side, a dead router is their ticket, and they will ask for the **circuit ID** before they look. The smartjack can loop the circuit so they test to the demarc without your router being involved. If their loop works and your ping across the WAN does not, the fault is your router, your cable to the NID, or your configuration.

The port is 100 Mbps. The contract promises 20. Traffic above 20 Mbps is **discard-eligible**. A speed test at midnight may show close to 100. The same test at noon, when the carrier's core is busy, drops the excess and your voice call is in that excess unless you marked it and they honor the mark. Shape your outbound traffic to 20 Mbps so you choose what gets delayed (9.1), instead of letting the carrier choose by dropping at random.

| What you bought | What failure looks like |
|---|---|
| Leased line | The circuit is yours. It is up, or it is down, at the CIR you pay for all month |
| MPLS | Each site is a port into a shared core. Your routes are in your VRF. A site learns the other sites through the carrier. There is no encryption unless you add it |
| Metro Ethernet | Your router sees an Ethernet link. The carrier's switches are invisible. A VLAN they handed you is their VLAN, and it must match |
| Internet VPN | Each site has ordinary Internet. The private path is the tunnel (8.3). When the Internet at the branch is congested, the tunnel is congested |
| Dark fiber | You supply the optics. A dirty connector or the wrong wavelength is your outage, and the carrier will tell you the fiber is fine |

A T1 is the leased-line shape the exam still describes in numbers: **24 × 64 kbps = 1.536 Mbps**, plus 8 kbps of framing, **1.544 Mbps**. Two copper pairs. A CSU/DSU on your end, a smartjack at the demarc. E1 is the same idea outside North America: **32 × 64 kbps = 2.048 Mbps**, of which 30 channels carry payload. You will not install one. You will recognize the rate and the box that terminates it.
